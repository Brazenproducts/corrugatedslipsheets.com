Slip Sheet Lip and Tab Design: Choosing One-Way, Two-Way, and Four-Way Pull

Corrugatedslipsheets Editorial • November 2026

The Lip Is the Interface

Every slip sheet decision eventually comes down to one detail that buyers frequently overlook on the first order: the lip, or pull tab, that the push-pull attachment grips. The lip is the mechanical interface between the sheet and the forklift, and getting its configuration wrong means slow cycle times, torn tabs, and frustrated operators. Procurement teams specifying reusable sheets for closed-loop systems should treat lip geometry as a primary spec line, not an afterthought.

One-Way, Two-Way, and Four-Way Pull

Slip sheets are produced with lips on one, two, or four edges, and the right choice depends on how loads are handled at each node of the supply chain. A one-way lip is the simplest and cheapest option and suits operations where every load is approached from a single, consistent direction. A two-way lip adds flexibility for facilities that pull from either of two opposing sides. A four-way lip, with a tab on every edge, is the most versatile and is preferred where operators cannot guarantee approach direction, such as busy cross-dock environments.

  • One-way: lowest cost, requires disciplined single-direction handling
  • Two-way: balances cost and flexibility for dual-approach lanes
  • Four-way: maximum handling freedom for high-mix, high-speed docks

Lip Length and Grip Reliability

Beyond edge count, lip length drives grip reliability. Too short and the push-pull gripper bar cannot get a secure bite, leading to slips and dropped loads; too long and the tab wastes material and can fold under itself during insertion. A typical lip runs between 2 and 4 inches depending on sheet gauge and load weight, and heavier loads generally warrant longer, higher-grammage tabs. When specifying, match the lip to the specific push-pull attachment model in your fleet, since gripper bar dimensions vary between manufacturers. Standardizing on well-made PP corrugated slip sheets with a consistent lip spec keeps your handling equipment calibrated to one reliable profile.

Gauge and Tab Durability

The lip is the highest-wear zone on a reusable slip sheet because it absorbs the full pull force on every cycle. A sheet that performs well across its field can still fail prematurely if the tab tears. This is where PP corrugated outperforms coated fiberboard: the polypropylene flute structure resists the repeated flexing and gripping that destroys paper tabs after a handful of uses. For demanding closed-loop programs, buyers should verify tab tear resistance during sample testing and consider a slightly heavier gauge concentrated at the pull edge.

Matching Sheets to Load Footprint

Lip configuration must be coordinated with overall sheet sizing. A four-way sheet only delivers its flexibility if each lip clears the load footprint cleanly, so the sheet dimensions have to account for both the product base and the added tab length on every gripped edge. Oversizing wastes material and creates overhang; undersizing leaves case edges unsupported. Teams running mixed load sizes often order several standardized footprints rather than forcing one size to cover everything.

Combining with Dividers for Unit-Load Integrity

For loads that need internal structure as well as a liftable base, lip-equipped sheets pair naturally with vertical inserts. Specifying a base sheet alongside PP divider boards and layer pads builds a complete reusable unit-load system from one material family, simplifying the bill of materials and ensuring consistent washdown and reuse behavior across every component.

Writing the Purchase Spec

A complete slip sheet spec should state the edge configuration, lip length, sheet footprint, wall thickness, areal weight, and the target reuse-cycle count. It should also reference the push-pull attachment model so the supplier can tune tab geometry to your equipment. Working with a direct manufacturer makes this coordination straightforward because lip length, edge count, and gauge can all be adjusted to the exact handling profile rather than selected from a limited catalog.

Conclusion

The lip is a small feature that determines whether a slip sheet program runs smoothly or stalls at the dock. By matching edge configuration to approach direction, sizing lip length to the gripper bar, and specifying a gauge that survives repeated pulls, procurement teams get reliable handling and long reuse life. Treat the pull tab as a first-class spec detail and the rest of the slip sheet program follows.
