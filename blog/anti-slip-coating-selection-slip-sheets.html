Selecting Anti-Slip Coatings for Corrugated Slip Sheets: A Load-Stability How-To

Corrugatedslipsheets Editorial • October 2026

Why Anti-Slip Coating Is a Load-Stability Decision, Not a Finish Option

When logistics and procurement teams evaluate corrugated slip sheets, surface friction is frequently treated as an afterthought. It should not be. The anti-slip coating on a slip sheet is the single factor that most directly governs whether a unit load stays intact during acceleration, braking, ramp travel, and over-the-road vibration. Selecting the right coefficient of friction (COF) is the difference between a stable 48-inch stack and a collapsed load on the dock. This guide walks buyers through specifying anti-slip performance on PP corrugated slip sheets in a way that matches real transport conditions.

Step 1: Understand Coefficient of Friction

Coefficient of friction measures the resistance to sliding between two surfaces. For slip sheets there are two interfaces that matter: the top surface against the product or its packaging, and the bottom surface against the pallet or push-pull platen. A higher COF on the product side keeps the load from shifting; a lower, controlled COF on the pull side allows the push-pull attachment to engage cleanly. A good anti-slip coating balances both.

  • Static COF resists the load starting to slide.
  • Dynamic COF governs behavior once movement begins.
  • Both are temperature-sensitive, so cold-chain loads need verified cold COF values.

Operations building closed-loop handling systems should start with PP corrugated slip sheets that publish both static and dynamic COF values rather than a vague anti-slip claim.

Step 2: Match COF to Your Load and Transport Profile

The required friction level scales with load weight, stack height, and how aggressive the transport environment is. Heavy, tall loads moving by truck over variable terrain need a higher product-side COF than light, short loads in a controlled warehouse shuttle. Export loads that experience marine roll and multiple handling events sit at the top of the requirement range.

  • Light warehouse loads: moderate COF is usually sufficient.
  • Over-the-road freight: higher COF to resist braking and lane-change forces.
  • Ocean export: highest COF plus edge control for multi-modal stability.

Document your worst-case scenario, not your average, so the coating is specified for the moment the load is most likely to fail.

Step 3: Evaluate Coating Types and Compatibility

Anti-slip performance can be delivered through surface texturing, applied coatings, or co-extruded high-friction layers. Each has trade-offs in durability, washability, and chemical compatibility. In sanitary environments the coating must survive washdown chemistry without breaking down or leaching, which is why coating selection and chemical resistance must be specified together.

  • Applied coatings: high initial friction, verify abrasion life over trips.
  • Textured surfaces: durable and washable, moderate friction gain.
  • Co-extruded layers: integral and long-lasting, ideal for reusable programs.

For applications with unusual load geometry or surface chemistry, a manufacturer offering custom corrugated plastic sheets can tune both the substrate gauge and the friction treatment to the load.

Step 4: Validate Before You Standardize

Never roll out a new slip sheet fleet-wide without a validation trial. Run representative loads through your actual transport lanes, including the most demanding routes and temperature swings. Measure load shift, tab durability, and push-pull cycle counts. Capture the data so the final spec is defensible and repeatable across future purchase orders.

Step 5: Build the Spec and Source It

Your anti-slip spec should state target static and dynamic COF values, the test temperature range, the coating type, washdown compatibility, and expected trip life. With those line items in hand, request quotes from manufacturers that can meet all of them rather than defaulting to the lowest unit price.
