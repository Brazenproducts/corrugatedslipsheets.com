Slip Sheet Sizing and Lip Overhang Guide for Palletizing

Corrugatedslipsheets Editorial • October 2026

Why Slip Sheet Sizing Drives Handling Reliability

Correctly sized slip sheets are the foundation of a dependable push-pull handling program. When the sheet footprint matches the load and the lip is set for the attachment, cycle times drop and product damage falls. When sizing is guessed, operators fight with slipping loads, torn lips, and loads that never seat cleanly on the receiving pallet. For procurement and logistics teams standardizing on PP corrugated slip sheets, specifying the right dimensions up front removes most of the friction that disposable fiber sheets introduce.

Matching the Deck Size to Your Load Footprint

The body of a slip sheet should match the unit load footprint it carries, not the pallet beneath it. For a standard 48x40 inch load, the usable deck is typically specified at 48x40, with the lip or lips added beyond that dimension. If your product overhangs or underhangs the pallet, size the deck to the actual load, then confirm the push-pull platen can seat fully under the sheet without catching adjacent loads in the aisle.

Underhang of more than an inch per side leaves product edges unsupported and invites crushing during transit. Overhang, where the sheet extends beyond the load, wastes material and snags on racking. Measuring the real palletized footprint, including shrink wrap, is the single most important step before placing an order.

Setting Lip Width for Push-Pull Attachments

The lip is the gripping tab the push-pull attachment clamps. Single-lip sheets suit simple pick-and-place lines, while two-, three-, and four-lip sheets support loads that must be pulled from multiple directions during cross-docking. Lip width is usually specified between 2.5 and 4 inches. Too narrow and the gripper bar cannot get a reliable bite; too wide and the lip folds under tension.

  • Single lip: inbound to outbound in one direction, lowest cost per sheet.
  • Two lips (opposite): load transferred and later pulled from the far side.
  • Four lips: maximum flexibility for mixed operations and automated lines.

Gauge and Flute Direction

Sheet gauge should scale with load weight and pull distance. Lighter case goods ride well on thinner twin-wall stock, while heavy bagged or boxed product needs a stiffer board that resists tearing as the gripper bar drags it across the deck. Flute direction matters too: aligning flutes with the primary pull direction maximizes tensile strength exactly where the pulling force concentrates. Pairing the correct sheet with PP divider boards and layer pads keeps stacked layers square and distributes load so the slip sheet is not asked to do work it was never specified for.

Stacking, Tier Sheets, and Multi-Layer Loads

In multi-layer palletizing, slip sheets double as tier separators. Size each intermediate sheet to the full layer footprint so the layer above sits flat and transfers load evenly to the one below. Mismatched tier sheets create rocking, which propagates up the stack and causes column failures in transit. Consistency across layers is what keeps tall, dense loads stable on the trailer.

A Practical Specification Checklist

  • Measure the real palletized footprint, including wrap and any overhang.
  • Choose deck size to match the load, not the pallet.
  • Select lip count and width based on how many directions the load is pulled.
  • Scale gauge to load weight and pull distance.
  • Align flute direction with the primary pull axis.
  • Confirm tier-sheet sizing for every layer in multi-layer loads.

Getting these six variables right converts slip sheets from a constant source of jams into a quiet, repeatable part of the operation. Spend the time measuring before you buy, document the spec, and reorder against it so every shipment behaves the same way on the dock.
